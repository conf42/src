# No tool will digitally transform you, and the user is the star

Conf42 Blog, 2023-03-30. https://www.conf42.com/blog/cloud2023/digital-transformation-end-user-is-the-star

In short: Zach Gardner (Keyhole Software) at Conf42 Cloud Native 2023 on four reasons digital transformations fail, from tool worship to the wrong people on the bus

Every vendor sells a digital transformation, and most of them fail for the same four reasons. At Conf42 Cloud Native 2023, [Zach Gardner](https://www.conf42.com/Cloud_Native_2023_Zach_Gardner_digital_transformation_fails__how_to_succeed), Chief Architect at Keyhole Software, lists them: the belief that a tool will do it, the belief that this time will be different, forgetting who the transformation is for, and the wrong people on the bus. His takeaway is blunt: the end user, not the CTO, is the star of the show.

He opens with a disclaimer that he is not there to sell a product, and the talk keeps that promise. It is a strategy talk with case studies, no code and no architecture diagrams.

## Why can't you just buy a transformation?

Reason one is the tool fallacy. "There is no single tool that will allow you to digitally transform your business," Zach Gardner said at Conf42 Cloud Native 2023. Two companies neck and neck in the same sector can work completely differently, so one off-the-shelf product cannot transform both.

He backs this with a short history of silver bullets, quoting each one's own book. Service-oriented architecture promised services that modelled the business so neatly that apps would compose themselves. The enterprise service bus moved the smarts into the pipes until nobody understood the pipes. Microservices came next, with their independent deployments and the outage nobody planned for. None of these is wrong, he argues, in the way a hammer is not wrong; the failure is treating one approach as the answer to everything.

## This time will not be different

Reason two follows from the first. After the purchase you wake up with the same team, the same habits and the same customers. So plan for it: define what digital transformation means for your business, then study companies that did it well and work out why their choices worked, rather than copying them.

His three case studies:

- **DHL** researched why customers picked competitors, found people want to know where their parcel is without talking to a human, and built a chatbot that tracks packages, reschedules deliveries and generates QR codes for labels. Most of its customers preferred mobile, so the bot works there too.
- **Disney** packed its parks with app features; the Genie trip planner let his family plan rides around two small children's nap schedule and the shortest queues. Disney executives also spend time each year in costume as cast members, living the customer's day.
- **Casey's** consolidated scattered customer systems onto Salesforce, growing a unified base of customer profiles toward 14 million from 300,000 nearly four years earlier, then used the data to find what customers loved most: the pizza. The result was a triple pepperoni pizza, which he counts as a digital transformation because the insight came from the data.

## Who is the transformation actually for?

Reason three is the one he cares about most. The mandate has to come from the top, but the transformation is felt at the bottom, by the customer. The case studies never name a heroic executive. "The end user is the star of the digital transformation," he said.

His practical tools are design sprints and ride-alongs, where the development team sits muted while real users talk through what they like and hate. CarMax does this well, he says. He cites a Forbes figure from 2015 that every $1 invested in UX brings $100 back, and a list that will sting anyone who has led a programme: put a BA in charge and you get a requirements doc, put an enterprise architect in charge and everything becomes an enterprise service, put a developer in charge and they want to rewrite it in last week's framework.

## The wrong people on the bus

Reason four is people. Inertia, in his words, is another word for culture, and someone who has done it one way for 15 years will keep doing it that way. The managers whose mental model never left the AS/400 may not be the right people for a programme that is about end users rather than any stack or SDLC. The right ones are not afraid of being wrong, work in cross-functional teams, and mix ideas from the ground, the executives and outsiders who have done it before.

## Who should watch

Architects, engineering managers and anyone handed a transformation budget line. Engineers will enjoy the SOA-to-microservices history lesson; leads will recognise the people problems. It is a talk about priorities, not about Kubernetes.
