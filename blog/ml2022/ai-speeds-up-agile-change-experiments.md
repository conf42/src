# Treat organisational change as small experiments, and let AI read the results

Conf42 Blog, 2022-05-19. https://www.conf42.com/blog/ml2022/ai-speeds-up-agile-change-experiments

In short: Felice Pescatore (Inspearit) at Conf42 Machine Learning 2022 on Intelligent Business Agility, using AI to pick and measure small organisational changes faster

Most agile transformations fail somewhere between the strategy deck and the Monday stand-up. At Conf42 Machine Learning 2022, Felice Pescatore, Agile Coach at [Inspearit](https://inspearit.it/), proposed Intelligent Business Agility (IBA): run organisational change as a series of small experiments, as lean change management already suggests, and use AI on the data those experiments produce to narrow the options and succeed faster. People keep the decisions; the models make the evidence easier to read.

This is a management talk with no code and no model architecture. The abstract also mentions a pilot called Arinn.ia, a digital agile master for teams, but the recorded talk doesn't present it.

## Business agility is three agilities, not one

Felice defines business agility as an organisation's ability to adapt to the market quickly and cost-effectively, and insists it rests on three pillars that are often confused with it. Enterprise agility is about people and leadership: going beyond rigid annual budgets that can't change, funding by value stream rather than by project, and thinking lean. Process agility is tailoring frameworks such as Scrum or Kanban to your goals instead of trying to be the world's best Scrum shop. Technical agility is excellence in practice and technology.

"There is no way to achieve the business agility without technical agility," Felice Pescatore said at Conf42 Machine Learning 2022.

## Change needs urgency, and an anthill

For leading the change itself he uses John Kotter's eight-step process and singles out the first step, creating a sense of urgency. When everything seems fine, nobody commits to changing; when things clearly aren't working, support comes easier. He isn't suggesting you manufacture a crisis, only that you watch for the right moment, because change has a cost and has to be motivated.

Then the disciplined approach to change, with an anthill as the metaphor: there's no single right framework, so every ant carries one small option. If an option fails, the failure is local and cheap, and someone tries a different one, informed by what didn't work. In practice that's lean change: define a starting model, plan a set of small experiments, ship each as a minimum viable change (MVC) with a metric attached, measure the outcome, then accelerate adoption, try a different MVC or change the plan.

## Where AI comes in

The measuring step is where his proposal lands. Experiments produce data, and people are not good at analysing large amounts of it. Machine learning and big data can suggest which options to pursue and cut down how many need trying. His definition of IBA, from the talk: the ability of an organisation to develop an adaptive culture supported by intelligent agents that help people make faster decisions, without removing the need for people.

He maps it back onto the three pillars. For enterprise agility, customer and social data to understand brand perception and pick innovation bets. For technical agility, checking whether technology choices actually fit the needed solution. For process agility, monitoring team maturity across sprints; his example is a bot that tracks how long dailies take and suggests ways to cut wasted time.

## Principles, stages and guardrails

Two principles underpin IBA: empowering and inclusive (a culture of sharing and collaboration, respecting privacy) and data-driven (everyone can reach the data, with rigorous data practices so decisions rest on good data). He describes four stages of adoption: foundation (low digitisation, basic analytics, often wrong expectations), approaching (digitisation under way, looking to optimise processes), aspirational (IBA tried and applied, a foundational data architecture being built) and mature (a continuous learning organisation that trusts the system).

The first guideline is STAIR: security and privacy, transparency, accountability, inclusiveness, reliability and safety. Accountability gets the most emphasis: suggestions from bots support decisions, and the people stay responsible for them. The second, AgileAI, is pragmatic: start with pre-built AI from the market rather than building from scratch, invest in AI development tools, build for people rather than for automation, and get your data estate AI-ready first.
