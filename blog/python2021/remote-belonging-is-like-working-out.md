# Belonging in a remote team is a workout, not a happy hour

Conf42 Blog, 2021-05-26. https://www.conf42.com/blog/python2021/remote-belonging-is-like-working-out

In short: Najeeb Khan (Teamland) at Conf42 Python 2021 on building belonging in remote teams, stage by stage, with activities chosen for a goal

You can sit on a Zoom call with 80 other people and still feel completely alone. At Conf42 Python 2021, Najeeb Khan, Head of People at Teamland, gives a short, practical talk on why remote teams drift apart and how to build belonging on purpose: treat it as an ongoing habit, match the activity to the stage your team is in, and start from a goal rather than a catalogue of games.

It's one of the shortest talks of the event, closer to a lightning talk than a lecture, and it isn't about Python at all. It is a people talk at a Python conference, and a useful one.

## Why does remote work feel so lonely?

Khan has worked remotely for eight-plus years and starts with why people chose it before the pandemic forced it: a flexible schedule, working from anywhere, no commute, time with family. Most companies now want to keep some form of remote work, and talent agrees; he says a third of people would flat out quit if forced back to the office full time. The abstract adds that 54% of employees feel unattached from their work and company.

The cost shows up in two places. Collaboration and loneliness, he says, make up 40% of the challenges remote workers report, and that's before time zones. Managers feel it too: 70% struggle with maintaining company culture and team cohesiveness. The tooling isn't the culprit. Slack, project boards and video calls keep everyone connected every minute; what's missing is the casual contact that used to happen by accident.

## Is there a quick fix?

No, and he leads with that. "Creating belonging isn't a one and done activity. In fact, it's like farming or working out," Najeeb Khan said at Conf42 Python 2021. The good news in the analogy is that, like exercise, it responds to steady effort, and there are many ways to do it.

## Match the effort to the team's stage

The useful framework in the talk is Tuckman's four stages of a team, with a different belonging lever for each:

- **Forming**: onboarding. The office version was a happy hour; remote, it has to be done purposefully, through activities led by employees or by the company.
- **Storming**: conflict resolution, which depends on empathy and understanding from managers and HR.
- **Norming**: cooperation, entertainment and deeper relationships, built through shared experiences, synchronous or asynchronous. Trivia or a virtual escape room both count, but he warns they aren't interchangeable; what matters is whether the time together actually builds trust.
- **Performing**: motivation, recognition and self-fulfilment, balancing intrinsic and extrinsic rewards. Neglect this stage and you get burnout. Praise and rewards help, but not as surface gestures.

One line he quotes from an attendee of a Teamland event sums up the inclusive part: they liked the party because there was no pressure to drink, dance or dress up. A shared struggle still needs to work for each person differently, whether that's a cooking class, yoga, meditation or a weekly games night.

## What should a manager actually do?

The question he hears most is which team-building activity to run. His answer is to start with the goal: getting to know each other casually, cooperation, motivation or plain entertainment. Once the goal is clear, the options narrow themselves. His budget estimate is concrete, five to ten hours a month, in exchange for productivity, mental health and wellbeing.

## Who should watch

Engineering managers and team leads running distributed teams, especially anyone who has noticed that the team ships fine but nobody talks outside stand-up. A few minutes, no code, one framework you can apply on Monday.
