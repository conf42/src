# Good work rarely gets you promoted on its own. Visibility does

Conf42 Blog, 2021-10-27. https://www.conf42.com/blog/js2021/raise-your-profile-to-get-promoted

In short: Lauren Hayward Schaefer (MongoDB) at Conf42 JavaScript 2021 on why developers must raise their profile to get promoted, and ten ways to do it

Tech likes to think of itself as a meritocracy, and the promotion committee does not read your pull requests. At Conf42 JavaScript 2021, [Lauren Hayward Schaefer](https://www.conf42.com/JavaScript_2021_Lauren_Hayward_Schaefer_Raise_Your_Profile), Staff Developer Advocate at MongoDB, argues that doing your job well is usually not enough to get promoted, and that raising your profile, inside the company and out, is work you can plan like any other.

It is a career talk, not a MongoDB product talk, and it comes with a worksheet you are meant to fill in while watching.

## What does raising your profile actually mean?

She starts with her own story. Asking her boss what it would take to get promoted, she was told to raise her profile internally: people outside her team needed to know who she was and what she did. As a remote employee who disliked networking, she asked a mentor for a plan, did not like the plan because it pushed her out of her comfort zone, and did it anyway. The promotion followed.

Profile, in her definition, is what people know or remember about you, in three parts: who you are (role, team, where you sit in the org), what you can do (technical and soft skills), and what you are known for. Her own example is honest about the mix: Node.js quickstarts, a schema design anti-patterns series, CI/CD, detail orientation, remote work advocacy and silly TikToks about developer life.

## Why bother, if the work should speak for itself?

Four reasons, and the first one is the strongest because it is written down. She looked at public engineering career frameworks and found influence in most of them:

- **Patreon** levels go from IC3 starting to broaden impact up to IC6 routinely having org- to industry-level impact.
- **Medium's** engineering growth rubric has a whole evangelism track, from representing Medium well externally to introducing it to audiences outside the industry.
- **Khan Academy's** career development document ties blogging and sharing work directly to market value and pay.

The other three: implicit requirements (beyond the lower levels, your manager cannot promote you alone, and the people who approve need to know who you are), doors opening (new roles, speaking invitations, leadership chances) and recruiting, since strong engineers want to work with visible ones.

## Ten ways to do it

1. Become the go-to person for a language, technology or customer problem. Dropbox's framework calls this being an authority on a domain.
2. Contribute to open source. If you do not want to code after hours, find a way to code in the open at work, and add a README to your GitHub profile.
3. Report the news for your team: represent it on a cross-functional group or curate a newsletter. Her caveat is that this can turn into admin work, so shape it for visibility.
4. Write blog posts, including about things you are still learning.
5. Give tech talks, starting with small lunch-and-learns.
6. Post on social media. Her TikToks did fine on TikTok and, to her surprise, much better when reposted on LinkedIn, where they reached MongoDB colleagues she rarely talked to.
7. Lead an employee networking group, if your company values that work. At IBM she led the worldwide work-from-home group.
8. Attend, host or speak at meetups and hackathons. Hosting needs no technical skills, just consistency.
9. Answer questions publicly online. At IBM she answered one forum question a day.
10. Advertise your work, which she calls possibly the most important.

## The compliments file

Number ten has the most practical habit in the talk. When someone praises a talk, she forwards the link to her manager; verbal compliments she asks people to send by email. Lauren Hayward Schaefer said at Conf42 JavaScript 2021: "I also have a Google Doc where I keep a list of every compliment that I receive." At review time she groups them and pastes them in, so her management team gets evidence instead of recollection. She also recommends finding a manager, or a boss's boss, who speaks well of you when you are not in the room.

She closes with a planning exercise: one action for this week, this month and this year, how you will measure success, and how you will hold yourself accountable. A calendar reminder to check in with yourself counts.

## Who should watch?

Mid-level developers who are doing good work and not getting promoted, and anyone who finds the word networking mildly painful. Senior engineers will know most of the list, but the career framework examples are useful when you need to explain the expectations to someone else.
