# A postmortem can be a detective story or a celebration

Conf42 Blog, 2021-02-24. https://www.conf42.com/blog/ce2021/five-kinds-of-postmortem

In short: Fabricio Buzeto (bxblue) at Conf42 Chaos Engineering 2021 on postmortems beyond failure - five types, from root cause analysis to open problem and celebration

Tech teams treat postmortems as paperwork for outages, which is why they wear such a grim face. At Conf42 Chaos Engineering 2021, [Fabricio Buzeto](https://www.conf42.com/Chaos_Engineering_2021_Fabricio_Buzeto_dia_de_los_muertos_postmortems), CTO at bxblue, argued that the format is wider than that: a postmortem can record a project's history, retrace an investigation, document an unsolved problem, or celebrate a win, and each of those helps a team learn.

It is a short talk and works best as a menu to pick from.

## What is a postmortem for?

Buzeto, coding since 2002 with time in big corporations, startups and academia, starts with definitions. In medicine a postmortem examines a body to find the cause of death; PMBOK defines it as a process to identify the causes of a project failure and how to prevent them. The counterpart is the pre-mortem, which looks forward to foresee and mitigate the causes of failure, and he points out you cannot do a good pre-mortem without learning from the past first.

His preferred definition comes from the synonym autopsy, which he traces to the Greek for seeing for yourself. Fabricio Buzeto said at Conf42 Chaos Engineering 2021: "It's a tool for learning. It's a tool for recording the history of a company, of a product, the tasks and the moments that were shared."

Seen that way, a postmortem is a communication tool: what one person lived through, others can learn from.

## The types

He says he classified six types, classic and new, and walks through these five:

- **Closure.** The classic PMBOK one, usually at the end of a project: what succeeded, so you can double down, and what failed, so you can avoid it next time.
- **Root cause analysis.** The one on tech blogs after downtime: why it happened and why it will not happen again, aimed at giving a sense of security.
- **Detective.** A first-person, step-by-step reconstruction of what happened and what the author was thinking. Others can learn from the reasoning, and the author can improve it.
- **Open problem.** Rare and useful: "It reports those findings of an ongoing issue or a pain point without an implemented solution yet." It invites others to help design the fix or the investigation, and if the problem returns months later, nobody starts from scratch.
- **Celebration.** Teams celebrate shared, predefined achievements, but each person has their own learning curve. Writing up why something mattered to one person recognises that different people on the team see things differently.

The detective and open problem types are the ones worth stealing. Most teams already write the first two; few write down an investigation in progress or a problem they have not cracked.

## How to start

His process is simple. Schedule time after a big event or anything worth recording, and write it down. Agree with the team where postmortems live, so people know where to look for context. Collect feedback, both to share what you learned and to catch what you missed. Share it, right away or later when something similar happens. Above all, just start; the rest falls into place once the habit exists.

The abstract mentions that his team grew from 5 to 18 people in two years while the application went from a few thousand to over a million users, and promises two occasions where postmortems paid for themselves. Those stories are not in the recording; the talk sticks to the types and the process.

## Who should watch

Engineering leads whose postmortems only appear after outages, and anyone who wants a lighter-weight way to document investigations. Pair it with a deeper process talk for the incident side.
