# Commit on day one - radical transparency for software teams

Conf42 Blog, 2025-03-07. https://www.conf42.com/blog/cloud2025/radical-transparency-for-software-teams

In short: Gregory Lind of Buildly at Conf42 Cloud Native 2025 on radical transparency, one shared backlog, open decisions, admitted mistakes and fewer silos.

New developers at Buildly are expected to commit code on their first day, into their own branch, and get feedback on it. At Conf42 Cloud Native 2025, [Gregory Lind](https://www.conf42.com/Cloud_Native_2025_Gregory_Lind_teams_collaboration_efficiency), CEO and founder of Buildly, used that ritual as the entry point to "radical therapy for software teams": a set of practices built on radical transparency, drawn from a book of the same name he co-wrote.

This is a culture talk, not a technical one, and it comes from a founder whose product (Buildly's AI-assisted product management, with humans in the loop) is built around the same ideas. No code on screen, a lot of hard-won management advice.

## Radical as in fundamental, therapy as in what managers end up doing

Lind defines his terms up front. Radical means a fundamental, far-reaching change, not the surfing kind. Therapy is a nod to what anyone who has managed a team already knows: a lot of the job is helping people through struggles and rebuilding trust and accountability. He is clear he is not a licensed therapist, he just plays one in product management.

## Four principles of transparency

The framework rests on open communication inside the team and with the wider organisation, data-driven decision making, continuous feedback from the team and later from customers, and inclusivity and diversity of perspectives. The practical move that ties them together is merging the product backlog with the developer backlog, so there is one process everyone can see. The payoff he claims: fewer silos, faster decisions, shorter conflicts (data settles arguments faster than seniority), and people who stay because they feel heard.

He points to Atlassian and to Google's habit of sharing engineers across cross-functional teams as examples, and to IBM's AI Fairness 360 and Netflix's responsible AI work as models for ethics tooling.

## Show your reasoning, not just your result

Transparency, in Lind's version, goes further than most teams are comfortable with:

- Share how a decision was made, not only what was decided, by opening the backlog to the team and even to customers.
- Ask for criticism, and keep it constructive inside the team.
- Make performance and compensation policies transparent, since that is where a lot of unhappiness actually comes from, along with reporting lines.
- Test AI features in production, not just the model that built them, and embed ethical checks in the process so nothing quietly skews what customers are shown.

## Run your product like a startup, and your startup like an open source project

The most memorable line in the talk is his summary of the method: run a product like a lean startup, and run a startup like an open source project. Share work openly, ask for feedback early, and build communities that contribute. He also defends building the better mousetrap: an existing tool that doesn't fit your needs is a reason to build in the open, not a reason to stop.

His counter-example is Theranos, built behind closed doors with fake-it-till-you-make-it secrecy and too much control in one person's hands. Transparency from the start, he argues, would have changed the story.

## Where Agile and Scrum fall short

Lind's critique of Scrum is that it separates roles (the pigs and chickens) when a cloud-native, multi-repo product needs everyone pulling together. His radical process brings development, product, marketing and design cycles into one, treats developers as stakeholders alongside management and marketing, and favours demo days and surveys over documentation for its own sake.

Two rules stand out. When you ship a bug that breaks part of the application, say so before users find it. And keep the CEO from going straight to individual developers; route questions through shared, consistently updated channels so nobody gets yanked out of focus.

He is also realistic about morale. The goal is not to be a cheerleader, but to build trust and safety, run daily check-ins people actually read, celebrate the occasional win, and notice who is struggling, including whether they are buried in too many tools.

## Who should watch

Engineering managers, product managers and founders of small teams who suspect their process has more walls than it needs. Engineers looking for technical content can skip it.
