# Conf42 MLOps 2026: a green check is not a fact

Conf42 Blog, 2026-11-20. https://www.conf42.com/blog/mlops2026/mlops-2026-roundup-green-checks-prove-nothing

In short: The Conf42 MLOps 2026 roundup - 53 talks on agents that fail quietly, evals that check what happened, identity for agents and governance in the pipeline.

[Conf42 MLOps 2026](https://www.conf42.com/mlops2026) premiered on November 19 with 53 talks, and the event's own tagline did most of the framing: training the model is the easy part. The hard part, according to speakers from Microsoft AI, Okta, JPMorgan Chase, Amazon, Walmart Global Tech, OpenAI, Aflac and a long list of smaller teams, is knowing whether the thing you shipped did what it says it did. Again and again the talks came back to the same uncomfortable observation. The request succeeded, the JSON parsed, the dashboard stayed green, and the result was still wrong.

Most of the program was about agents, but the classic MLOps work was well represented too: training data, labels, edge inference, GPUs and pipelines that auditors can replay.

## Agents fail quietly, so the checks have to look elsewhere

[Muhammad Haris Farooq](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Muhammad_Haris_Farooq_agents_patterns_production) of Ruby AI runs more than 50 types of agents and about 3,800 production runs a month, and he opened with a bug where nothing was broken: prompt caching was switched on, yet the cache hit rate sat at 0% for months because the code flattened the system prompt into one string and dropped the cache markers. "They don't fail loudly, they fail quietly, and they still give you something that looks fine," Muhammad Haris Farooq said at Conf42 MLOps 2026. His fixes are plain engineering: math, formatting and data changes go to code, every run is priced, and drawing the dependency graph of 13 sequential agents took a research job from a median of 32.5 minutes to about 10.7.

[Danila Redikultsev](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Danila_Redikultsev_evidence_agent_checks) of Aeroclub gave the theme its name. In his rebuilt support store, a refund passes every policy check and still goes to the wrong order, because the one value the check never questioned, the order ID, was picked by the model. He calls that self-signed evidence, and a second model did not save him: asked to review 72 wrong refunds, an LLM judge approved every one. "A green check is not a fact. It's a question. Who signed it?" Danila Redikultsev said at Conf42 MLOps 2026.

Several talks cover the same ground from the guardrail side. [Dylan Merigaud](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Dylan_Merigaud_rules_agent_code) of Merigaud Consulting compares rules written as sentences with rules enforced in code on the path of the action, from a preregistered replay of 5 rules and 200 runs. [Michael Oskola](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Michael_Oskola_agent_fleet_gate) of Retailbox shares the build log of an agent fleet where every fact passes a write-gate before it can be remembered, and [Shipra Sonal and Madhu Gurramkonda](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Shipra_Sonal_Madhu_Gurramkonda_validator_agents_pattern) of Amazon describe a validator that checks every claim in a draft answer against its live source.

## Evals that check what actually happened

[Sajal Garg](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Sajal_Garg_agent_evals_happened) of Microsoft AI split an agent run into three records that can disagree: what the reply claims, which tool the agent tried to use, and what the service says changed. His worked example, a training job that must be submitted once and only after approval, turns into a repeatable test where the reply is an input to the check and never its own answer key.

[Reza Amini](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Reza_Amini_agent_replay_logs) of Flashback attacked the step before the eval: reproducing the failure. His warehouse agent reserves three pallets when the customer ordered three cartons, and rerunning it live would hit the real systems again and might take a different path. His answer is record, replay, fork: save every model and tool call to a file, replay it offline, then fork from the first wrong call and run live only from there.

The abstract-only half of the evals track points the same way. [Dylan Ler](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Dylan_Ler_evals_agents_deployment) of Adobe argues for evals as a deployment artifact, [Raju Dandigam](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Raju_Dandigam_trace_regression_agent) of Navan turns an agent trace into a regression test that runs in CI, [Anwar Khan](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Anwar_Khan_eval_llms_harness) of John Deere covers eval-as-code, and [Ocean Coldiron](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Ocean_Coldiron_evals_clinical_configuration) of ModMed tests an LLM that writes clinical configuration, where a wrong answer becomes a patient-safety ticket.

## Who is calling, and on whose behalf?

The security talks were mostly about identity. [Bhavik Thakkar](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Bhavik_Thakkar_identity_authorization_agents) of Okta started from the shared API key that most ML platforms still run on. "If you have the key, you are in, and nobody can tell one caller from another," Bhavik Thakkar said at Conf42 MLOps 2026. His bar for every model call is three questions: who is calling, on whose behalf, and allowed to do what, answered through five building blocks with a SPIFFE example.

[Ajay Munjuluru](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Ajay_Munjuluru_permission_authorization_evals) of JPMorgan Chase pointed out that agent eval suites score accuracy, helpfulness and cost, and none of those checks care who is asking. He tests for permission leaks with personas, takes the expected results from the source system instead of from the test author, and lets a failure block the release. [Nishanth Vepachedu](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Nishanth_Vepachedu_microservices_observability_llms) made the sharpest version of the argument for incident agents: diagnosis is the easy bit and authority is the hard part, because such an agent holds production privileges while reading logs that anyone who can send a request helped write.

[Kriti Faujdar](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Kriti_Faujdar_securing_agents_infrastructure) of Yelp mapped the attack surface as four layers, from the model through agents and tools down to infrastructure, and [Jose Manuel Ortega](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Jose_Manuel_Ortega_security_factory_mlops) of Alicante University covered security strategies for the MLOps pipeline itself.

## Governance becomes a pipeline stage

[Shanker Valipireddy](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Shanker_Valipireddy_agentic_regulated_enterprise) of Aflac brought the regulated-enterprise view in three lessons: give every agent an identity, gate actions by risk instead of by hope, and treat evaluation as a release gate that has to keep passing. [Arpita Sheth](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Arpita_Sheth_compliance_governance_pipeline) started with an auditor's email asking what data trained the production model, who approved it and how it has behaved since launch, then built the answer in five layers on top of the registry, CI/CD and monitoring a team already has. [Ankur Mehra](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Ankur_Mehra_loan_valuation_pipelines) applied the same discipline to valuing loans that have no market price: every ingestion batch gets a timestamp and a hash, and rejected data goes to a dead letter queue instead of into a number someone signs off on.

From the abstracts, [Ankur Gupta](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Ankur_Gupta_rules_engine_governance) of Roblox and OWASP presents a rules engine for AI use case governance with policy defined in YAML, [Joao Marques](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Joao_Marques_audit_tamper_agents) of Asqav covers hash-chained, signed audit trails for agents, and [Neelima Verma](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Neelima_Verma_fairness_compliant_harm) measures fairness in dollars on 95,588 real mortgage applications.

## From a demo to a page with real traffic

[Artem Zamarajev](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Artem_Zamarajev_llm_commerce_renders) of Amazon talked about LLM features that live on a web page, with a made-up coffee store as the running example. His first two lessons are to precompute by default and to keep the model off the critical path, with a fallback ladder decided before anyone writes a prompt: fresh content, then the last good version, then a plain non-AI default. [Meher Bhaskar Madiraju](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Meher_Bhaskar_Madiraju_agentic_poc_deploying) of Walmart Global Tech set model capability against outcomes and went through the failure modes between a proof of concept and production one by one.

The inference track goes below the API. [Alessandro Vozza](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Alessandro_Vozza_token_silicon_agents) of Volt Datacenters follows a single token from an agent in Kubernetes to vLLM on bare-metal GPUs and back, [Kosseila Haddalene](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Kosseila_Haddalene_vllm_kubernetes_gpu) of Cloudthrill shares a Terraform-driven vLLM stack for EKS, AKS, GKE and Nebius, and [Nishant Modak](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Nishant_Modak_gpu_workload_metric) of Last9 joins GPU telemetry to the Kubernetes workload that owns it. [Phoebe Wang](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Phoebe_Wang_codex_flyte_mcp) of OpenAI showed a narrow, read-only Flyte MCP tool that lets Codex look up a failed training run from its link and say which step broke.

## Data, labels and small models still decide the result

The least fashionable result of the event came from [Vladimir Beskorovainyi](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Vladimir_Beskorovainyi_noisy_trie_labeling) of Besk Tech: on noisy product names from receipts, a character n-gram logistic regression beat a 1D CNN and an LSTM in 30 cells out of 30 on his public synthetic benchmark. It was also the slowest of the cheap models, which is where his talk gets interesting.

[Devavrat Khanolkar](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Devavrat_Khanolkar_containers_vulnerability_inference) of Cisco Systems explained exposure bias in ranking systems, where items a user never saw get logged as impressions and end up as false negatives in the training data. [Nicolo Brandizzi](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Nicolo_Brandizzi_llm_training_documents) of Fraunhofer IAIS gave a beginner's tour of how web-scale text becomes LLM training data, and [Deborah Adedigba](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Deborah_Adedigba_skin_lesion_classification) of the UK Ministry of Defence asked what you need to see before anyone relies on a skin lesion classifier that scores well in a notebook. At the edge, [RanjithKumar Ragavan](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_RanjithKumar_Ragavan_executorch_pytorch_edge) of T-Mobile traced a six-kilobyte network from PyTorch training to on-device Android inference with ExecuTorch in his personal open source project, and [Layth Ayache](https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Layth_Ayache_dataset_benchmark_sign) of Achour Holding covers the MLOps under a Lebanese Sign Language dataset of more than 40,000 samples that had to be built from nothing.
