# Innovation starts with naming the problem, and creativity needs constraints

Conf42 Blog, 2020-08-26. https://www.conf42.com/blog/sre2020/innovation-problem-type-constraints-team-games

In short: Amber Vanderburg (The PathWayz Group) at Conf42 Site Reliability Engineering 2020 on framing problems, balancing constraints and remote team exercises

Most innovation efforts fail at the start, by not saying which problem they're solving. At Conf42 Site Reliability Engineering 2020, Amber Vanderburg, founder of The PathWayz Group, argued that teams should first name the type of problem, then build a balanced environment for creative work, and she brought a set of quick exercises that work on a Zoom call.

It's the second of her two talks at the event (the other covers [building and leading remote teams](https://www.conf42.com/Site_Reliability_Engineering_2020_Amber_Vanderburg_remote_teams)), and like that one it's about people, not systems. It's also unusually hands-on: she tells you to pause the video and draw.

## What kind of problem is it?

She opens with a game: in 15 seconds, find two things at home starting with the first letters of your first and last name. Hers were an apple and a vinyl record. It cuts screen fatigue and brings personality back into meetings.

Then the framework. Teams solve three types of problems. Fixing an existing system, process or product calls for causal analysis down to the root cause. Improving something that already works calls for value engineering: find what's good and build on it (which is why phones come in numbered generations). Creating something new is the Edison, Tesla and Disney category. Each needs different methods, so name the type first.

Asking the right question is a small pivot. What is five plus five has one answer; what two numbers equal ten has many. And she suggests asking why or what three to five times to reach the root cause. In her experience, the biggest oversight in innovation efforts is at the very beginning: not identifying the problem.

## What makes an environment where people do their best work?

Balance, in three places. First, flow, the psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's concept: a challenge slightly above your current ability. If you can run one mile, a 15-mile target is disheartening; if you run ten, half a mile is boring.

Second, the framework. Leaders should define the what and the why, and give the team ownership of the how. "True creativity actually thrives with some constraints," Amber Vanderburg said at Conf42 Site Reliability Engineering 2020. No constraints limits creativity; too many frustrates people.

Third, feedback loops. If every idea in a brainstorm is shot down before it's finished, people stop sharing. If nothing gets analysed until an idea is fully built, you've wasted the work. Let ideas be voiced, then critique them while still in ideation.

## How do you read team dynamics on a video call?

Her exercises each take a couple of minutes:

- Lean left or right. In gallery view, lean right if you're comfortable with confrontation, left if not. Then calls versus email, morning versus evening, public versus private praise. If most of the team prefers email and you phone everyone, or you schedule mornings for evening people, you'll see it in under a minute.
- Circles in 90 seconds. Draw as many circular objects as you can. Some people draw 15 or 20 simple ones, others six or eight detailed ones. Neither is better, and a team needs both.
- Strengths card. One side, the strength you bring to the project; the other, a strength you hope someone else has. Amber, who calls herself a big-picture person, always asks for someone detail-oriented. The cards show who will complement whom, and where friction will come from.

## Why talk about conflict before there is any?

The cards feed what she calls proactive conversations, held at the start of a project or client relationship while emotions are low. She opens with the admission that at some point in the project she's going to fail you, then agrees with the other person on how they'll handle it. It normalises conflict and leaves a plan for when it arrives.

Her chain of reasoning: more ownership brings more chances for conflict, and more conflict brings more conversation instead of complacency. Healthy, innovative teams engage in healthy conflict.

The story behind it comes from coaching young footballers at Adidas and Paris Saint-Germain academies in Bengaluru. Players drilled to stand in line, kick, and wait for instruction did the drill perfectly, then turned to the sideline mid-game for directions. Replacing drills with challenges (knock down three cones, which needs power and precision) produced fierce debates among seven-year-olds over laces versus instep. Both were right, and the players owned the how.

## What's the last exercise?

Fold a page into eight boxes and draw a different dog in each, 15 seconds per box when the timer beeps. Her client was a vet clinic whose mascot dog looked like it was frowning. Post the boxes in a Slack or Teams channel, comment in threads, combine the best parts. For people who like to mull things over, she sends the prompt a few days early.
