# If the thread needs more than six emails, pick up the phone

Conf42 Blog, 2020-08-27. https://www.conf42.com/blog/sre2020/remote-teams-four-pillars-six-emails

In short: Amber Vanderburg (The PathWayz Group) at Conf42 Site Reliability Engineering 2020 on building, valuing, training and retaining remote teams

Remote teams run on the same four pillars as office teams (build, value, train, retain), but each one needs different tools once the team lives in a grid of video tiles. At Conf42 Site Reliability Engineering 2020, Amber Vanderburg, founder of The PathWayz Group, which trains teams worldwide in leadership, team building and strategic HR, gave a practical talk for managers who went remote overnight in 2020.

It's a leadership talk at an SRE conference, with no tooling or architecture in it. The payoff is a handful of habits you can apply the next morning.

## Which kind of remote team do you have?

Amber starts by separating them. Globally remote teams are more diverse and bring a wider network, but add cultural communication, collaboration and time-zone problems; she leads teams like that. Domestically and locally remote teams work from home in the same general area. Hybrid teams split time between home and office. Knowing which you lead shapes policy, leadership style and culture.

## Build: hire humble, hungry and smart

Her first pillar is recruitment, which she calls how organisations build the future. She leans on Patrick Lencioni's ideal team player and adapts each trait to remote work.

Humble people are trainable, adaptable and admit mistakes, which matters because misunderstandings across cultures are far more likely remotely. Ask candidates about working across cultures and communication styles and how they handled a miscommunication. Hungry people want to improve and have a strong work ethic; since remote work is largely self-driven, ask what they've worked hard for and how they keep learning new technology. Smart means high IQ and high EQ: able to do the job, and people-smart enough to build the connections remote teams lack. She notes the skills differ, too. Someone who dresses for success but writes poor email may struggle more remotely than in an office.

Miss one trait and it shows. Hungry and smart without humility is arrogance; humble and smart without hunger does the job and nothing more; humble and hungry without smarts works hard at the wrong level. Her homework: reread your interview questions.

## Value: tools, benefits and actual contact

People want to feel valued, and the first proof is the right tools for communication, project management and HR. Saying you care about success while withholding them is frustrating. She recommends separate work phones and email accounts from onboarding, for a clear line between work and home and a clean offboarding later.

On pay, she flags the open debate about adjusting for remote workers' cost of living. On benefits, her advice is simply to ask your team what they want; office-style perks mean little remotely. And because isolation hits some remote workers hard: care packages, check-ins, themed dress-up days on Zoom, happy hours, game nights. She has hosted game shows for her teams.

## Train: practice between Sundays

Her sports analogy: sign the best players at the highest salaries, never practise, and you won't win. Train between Sundays to compete on game day. Virtual meetups and continuing education matter, and so do in-person meetings, annual or twice yearly for global teams and monthly for some local ones. Training is where hungry people get to grow.

## Retain: clear communication, and a ladder

Remote work isn't a moat. Amber puts it bluntly: you're not the only team that offers remote work. The thread through every pillar is communication, and she borrows Dr. Mark Rutland's frame: the right message, to the right audience, in the right way, at the right time.

The method gets the most attention, and here's the rule worth stealing. With most teams she works out how many emails or messages a problem should take before someone calls or opens a Zoom; usually five to seven, about six on average. If a thread will need ten or fifteen, email is the wrong channel. You get the answer faster, and you avoid the gaps between replies in which tension builds. The reverse holds too: some meetings should be a memo.

On timing and tone: "let us not conflate our brief communication with clear communication," Amber Vanderburg said at Conf42 Site Reliability Engineering 2020. A terse message that needs three follow-ups saved nobody time. Add growth paths, horizontal as well as vertical, with a rough timeline, and people have reasons to stay.

She closes with a story from coaching young players at Paris Saint-Germain academies in Bengaluru: a six-year-old shouting pass all game, because he only knew it as a football word. Leaders shouting recruitment, retention and innovation without a plan sound much the same.
