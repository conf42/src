From Prompts to Review Harnesses: Scaling AI Code Reviews Across 70+ Software Projects

Guido SchefflerExecutive Consultant @ CGI

    Hi, welcome to my presentation, From Prompts to Review Harnesses Scaling AI Code Reviews Across 70+ Software Projects.

    Who am I? I'm Guido. I'm an executive consultant at CGI. I've been working in the industry for 15 years or so, and have been with CGI for a few of those Now, what are we going to talk about today? First of all, why did we even do what we did? Then what was our initial approach? What problems we came across, how we solved them, what the results of that whole process were, what did we learn along the way, and what our next steps are.

    But why AI reviews? We had critical manual findings in one project in the middle of last year, and as a result, we needed to review seventy plus more projects. The issue was one of those manual reviews took a person like two or three days of work, and we simply didn't have enough budget or people for those manual reviews So we knew we had to turn to AI.

    But why did we build our own tool? First of all, compliance. We simply couldn't use a public API from Anthropic or OpenAI for that client of ours. Then we wanted better long-term cost control, so if one of the model providers simply decides to jack up the prices, we can just switch the model or the model provider or even both. Then we wanted complete model independence. Complete meaning we technically wanted to be able to switch to any model at any time. There are obviously quality differences, and we can't just use any model, but technically we could. And then we wanted better control over the review criteria because, as I said, we had that specific critical finding, and we needed to check a lot of projects about this finding, so we needed to make sure that the review process actually looks for that specific finding So we started with our initial approach being a simple prompt-based approach. We used OpenCode as an agent runner.

    OpenCode because we had previous experience with that and code reviews are code anyway, so that might just work. So we had one prompt, one detailed task markdown, writing down exactly what we wanted the agent to do in the review, then multiple checklists. A checklist in that sense being a cross-project list of review criteria by project type, topic, or technology stack, and one report template so the agent knows what the final report should look like.

    And the results look good at first, because everything that comes out of an LLM looks good at first Which brings us neatly to our first set of problems and solutions. So the prompt-based approach had some problems. The agents just wouldn't perform consistent, complete reviews. They forgot to read files, they forgot checklist items. They just didn't really follow the report template. And what we initially thought that it might be the context window, that it's just too much context at once. So we introduced some sub-agents or instructed open code to specifically spawn sub-agents for certain tasks, which did help, but it just didn't solve the problem. And one of the highlights I always bring up is a review where the agent afterwards said, "I had this 700-file project and read 11 files, and now I give you the whole report over the full project." Which of course is completely useless because even the most skilled human or agent could not give you a full report for a project by reading only 11 files of it But those weren't all the issues.

    We had a very interesting problem when we switched from Claude Sonnet 4.5 to 4.6, where the agent suddenly started actively ignoring large parts of the repository even though the instructions clearly said that it is very important to review everything. We simply had to face the truth that trying to force it through the prompt is simply not reliable, and we really tried a lot there.

    We, of course, also asked the agents why they did that, so we asked basically for rationalization, which was not particularly useful, but it was fun, and that's why I want to share two of those quotes with you. First of all, "I understood that the task was to look at everything, but decided that the review was good enough after looking at half of it." And then, "I consider looking at these files irrelevant in the context of the review, even though that clearly violates the rules." Of course, those are not the exact quotes. They are paraphrased slightly because the LMS produce rationalizations that are, say, a lot more wordy than those short quotes. But either way, in the end, we knew that with our prompt-based approach, we were at an end. We just couldn't continue further. It was just not reliable to go down that path.

    But how do you work with someone or something that seemingly doesn't only know the concept of malicious compliance, but has fully internalized it?

    We thought we needed a deterministic process and some enforcement.

    First of all, we looked for something where we could take our review steps from the task markdown file into a deterministic review process, and for that, we found Strends Agents. Strends Agents SDK is a really nice way to work with agents, by the way.

    Then we took the steps that were programmatically solvable, something like reading in the checklist items, generating the final report from the review templates, and stuff like that.

    Next, we looked at the steps that the LLMs should solve, and for those, we gave the agents only the very specific tools that they needed for that step. For example, when they are checking if a checklist is applicable for the project, they don't have to have access to tools for issue management and stuff like that. And also for those steps, we had a very focused prompt so the agents know exactly what they should do.

    Finally, each step has a validation stage, and without a successful validation, the process does not move to the next step.

    Most of you are probably thinking right now, "Wait a minute. That man in the video, isn't he talking about a harness?" And yes, I am talking about a harness. But at the time where we had that idea, at the end of last year, that term really wasn't widespread.

    The important thing, though, is did it solve our problems? And yes, it did, mostly. By taking programmatic control over checklists and files, we reduced hallucinations and forgetting of tasks dramatically. We could enforce the completeness by requiring the individual results to be handed over to the programmatic process.

    And since the report was now generated programmatically, it did finally adhere to the templates we set. And if for some reason a result was missing, we could just send the agent back to the necessary step and make it fix their mistake.

    Now this process, of course, needs some kind of working memory. So behind the scenes, we went ahead and carefully designed a memory file storage, which, for one, serves obviously as the raw data behind the report, but also helps us achieve future goals like resuming interrupted reviews, at some point maybe incremental reviews, and, as a bit of foreshadowing, later processing of the report data Speaking of the reports, we of course needed to make the results accessible, and we hit a roadblock there pretty much immediately. While during the test runs, we gave the reports to some people to evaluate them, and they obviously read the whole reports in the production stage. Pretty much no one wanted to read a large markdown report, and they were really large. So our first step there was to break that off into multiple focused report templates, which helped a bit, but the LLM output is still very wordy, and so no one really wanted to read that either.

    It dawned on us that markdown and text are inherently just too difficult to read. So we slop-coded a visualization tool and integrated it into our quality dashboard, which worked perfectly fine. The developers actually interacted with the reports, clicked through the files, through the issues, through the checklist items. They could filter them. They were happy. They really used that tool and gave us absolutely positive feedback Riding on this high, we thought, how could we make the developers even happier? And we created an MCP server for interacting with the report results right in the IDE. And that MCP server worked perfectly fine, but in the end, no one used it. Okay, I used it, and it really worked very well, but the developers just didn't want to use it. They wanted the report. They wanted to read it, take the information out of it that they needed for their specific task, and then get along with it and not chat to a, an LLM to solve the problem for them.

    Even with that minor setbacks, we still have the results that pretty much speak for themselves.

    We started in that whole operation with two to three days of manual effort for one review, and we reduced that to one to two hours plus twenty euros in tokens, which is an amazing reduction.

    Of course, we all know that LLMs make funny and/or stupid mistakes, and honestly, that happens in our process, too. But we still feel that we are at ninety percent of the review quality a human expert would provide in two to three days. And those ten percent, developers just ignore them and take the value out of the remaining ninety percent.

    Combined with the efficient result interaction we built, we got almost exclusively positive feedback. And while it was true that when I submitted that talk, we had seventy-plus projects, I can happily say that up until now, we have created immense value for over four hundred projects with that process.

    But what's probably more important to you is what did we learn along the way? First of all, even good prompts are no guarantee, especially the longer ones. The longer a prompt is, the less likely the agent actually follows all of its instructions.

    And even if they seemingly do, LLMs tend to follow instructions while missing the point. For example, very early on in the process, we had a requirement that in the memory files at the end, every file in the repository needs to be marked as reviewed. Then the LLM at some point said those are a lot of files, and I don't think there's anything important in there, so I just take a batch job and mark every file in a specific category as reviewed without actually reviewing the file," which is, of course, fully missing the point.

    Combining that with the strange behavioral change from CloudSonnet four point five to four point six means that LLM outputs should be continuously evaluated if you want to make sure that your process quality stays the same or at some point, maybe even improves. We also think that a harness is essential. But to be fair, nowadays, this shouldn't really be news to anyone.

    What is still more widespread is to just throw everything at the LLM. But if a script can do the job, a script really should do the job. Of course, you can write the script with an LLM, but once the script is written, it doesn't incur any more cost for each run, and it can also be tested deterministically, making it easier to uphold a certain level of quality. In the end, both LLMs and scripts are tools which need to be used properly.

    While not being a major learning, it's probably interesting for you what kind of model performance we observed. With the Anthropic models, Haiku was just not reliable with tool usage and also has a way too small context window, so in the end it was basically unusable for that task. Sonnet, on the other hand, was the cost-benefit sweet spot. Opus, while being slightly better than Sonnet when looking at the results, was way slower and ten times more expensive. And those ten times aren't just a figure of speech, it's actually ten times more expensive in our testing.

    As for local models, and I'm talking about my M4 Max MacBook Pro here with thirty-six gigabytes of unified memory, they do work in principle, but they are very slow. And not only that they are slow, they also have a big quality bottleneck, the context window size.

    So at least with the 27B models I can run in my machine, the quality is significantly below Sonnet and therefore sadly not usable right now. But with the advancements in that field and the much beefier hardware we ordered, that may very well change in the near future.

    Speaking of that near future, what are our next steps here? We of course want to evaluate new models, especially local ones, as they come out.

    We also want to try to introduce tools for deterministic structural analysis, like Graphify, into the process to further improve token and time efficiency. Then, especially when we go into the incremental reviews direction, we want to introduce checklist and findings management so people can properly work with reviews over a long period of time. Finally, we may look into creating pull requests and fixes automatically. However, currently this is out of scope for the use cases we built that process for. And with that, I want to thank you for your time and hope the insights from this presentation were valuable to you.

    Abstract

    How do you turn brittle AI prompts into a reliable review process for many software projects? This experience report shows the evolution from prompt-based AI reviews to a deterministic review harness with an agent runner, MCP server, managed checklists, enforced completeness, persisted intermediate results, automated reports, and an interactive result UI.
