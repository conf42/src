    Abstract

    Platform teams building shared LLM evaluation services face a practical cost question: should each criterion get its own judge call, or should one call return several scores? Packing criteria reduces repeated input, but a cheaper evaluation service is only useful if its measurements remain informative. I built an experiment that keeps the criteria constant while varying how many share a call. The design randomizes criterion grouping and order, preserves the output budget per criterion, and tracks agreement with human labels alongside tied scores. In the documented five-criterion pilot, cost per thousand criteria fell from $0.593 to $0.318, with no statistically detectable accuracy change. That finding is specific to the comparison; it is not proof of equivalence or a universal packing limit. This talk walks through the experimental controls, a rubric-anchoring failure, and the limits of extending the study with synthetic corruptions. Attendees will leave with a method for testing a packing change before adopting it in a shared evaluation pipeline, including what to hold constant, which quality and cost measures to report, and when the evidence is too weak to justify rollout.
