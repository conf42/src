# Trust me, bro? Is not a security stratergy in an agentic ai framework.

A talk at [Conf42 Chaos Engineering 2026](https://www.conf42.com/chaos2026) by [Bongani Mhlanga](https://www.conf42.com/speaker_Bongani_Mhlanga) (Senior Software Engineer @ Sync Access). Page: https://www.conf42.com/Chaos_Engineering_2026_Bongani_Mhlanga_security_agentic_ai

## Abstract

Every product team is being asked to add "our own AI". The demo takes a day; the trouble starts after that. I follow Skhumba, a fictional delivery company's AI assistant, from demo to production across eight layers of a real AI stack: model, system prompt, retrieval, tools (MCP), access control, guardrails, cost control and observability. Each fix creates the next problem. It recommends a competitor. It invents a refund limit because it never saw the policy. Once it has tools, it reads payroll data to a new hire and obeys a hidden instruction in a customer email. Then 2,000 people ask at once and the token bill explodes. Even a Friday prompt change that skips the evals brings the first bug back. At every failure the audience decides what to do, then we see the fix and what it costs. The scenarios are illustrative, built to show patterns developers will meet, not measurements from one production system. As AI agents join everyday business workflows, trust alone is not enough, and "trust me, bro" is not a security strategy. This session turns AI governance, security and responsible adoption into concrete engineering decisions a team can make on Monday.  What attendees take away 1.Who the agent acts as and what it may touch: narrow tool permissions and human approval for consequential actions reduce the damage when an injection gets through. Filters and detection alone do not make injection safe. 2.How to test prompt and model changes before they ship, and why the change that skips the evals is the one that brings old bugs back. 3.How to trace failures across model, retrieval, tools and cost - investigating inputs, tool calls and failures - and who owns the system when it acts badly.
