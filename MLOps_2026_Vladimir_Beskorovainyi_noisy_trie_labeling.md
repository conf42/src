# Coding Noisy Product Names for Consumer Price Statistics: Trie Rules, Bag-of-Words and Reliability-Weighted Labeling

A talk at [Conf42 MLOps 2026](https://www.conf42.com/mlops2026) by [Vladimir Beskorovainyi](https://www.conf42.com/speaker_Vladimir_Beskorovainyi) (CTO & CPO @ Besk Tech). Page: https://www.conf42.com/MLOps_2026_Vladimir_Beskorovainyi_noisy_trie_labeling

## Abstract

We ran a 1D-CNN, an LSTM and a character n-gram logistic regression head to head on the same noisy-product-name task, under one matched training protocol. The logistic regression won every category. This talk is about why, and about what that means for anyone classifying short, messy text.  The setting is consumer price measurement from alternative data: scanner exports, web-scraped listings, transaction and receipt records. They promise broader coverage, and they arrive as short, abbreviated, inconsistently spelled product names with no standard product code. Before any price can be compared, every item has to be mapped to a consumption classification such as COICOP. The method has separately been deployed in production on data that cannot be released; everything measured in this talk comes from the public benchmark.  I will go through the pipeline step by step: domain-specific normalization and tokenization of noisy item names, a prefix-tree (trie) pre-classifier driven by per-category key phrases and stop phrases, and a per-category binary confirmation model that decides whether an item really belongs to the category the rules assigned. I will explain why binary confirmation per category beats one multiclass model here, and what that choice costs you.  One operational lesson worth the ticket on its own: rules look like they are solving the problem, and then they are not. They cover only 32 to 50 percent of items, and coverage erodes as new product names appear. The learned stage is permanent infrastructure, not a stopgap you remove once the rule set matures.  Labels are the other half. The protocol has several annotators giving a binary valid/reject judgment, with votes aggregated by a reliability weight that updates as annotation proceeds. The model sits inside the same rule, so fine-tuning is continual rather than a periodic project. Around 66 labeled examples per category turn out to be enough.  Then the numbers: mean F1 0.997 for the character n-gram logistic regression, word-order features adding nothing, the neural baselines behind. The benchmark is synthetic and public, and I will say plainly what that does and does not establish.  Takeaways: where the bottleneck in noisy short-text classification actually sits, how to structure annotation so it compounds instead of decaying, and how to recognize the case where reaching for a larger, order-sensitive model is the wrong move.
